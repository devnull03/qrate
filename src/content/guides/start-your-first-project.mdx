By the end of this tutorial you will have your own qrate project open, with your collection's rows
in it, saved as one `.qrate` file. It takes about ten minutes, and most of that is deciding which
way to start.

## Install qrate

Download qrate from the [install page](/docs/install). The recommended **base** download suits
most people: it is small, and qrate offers to add optional tools, such as PDF and video previews,
the first time you need them. On a Mac you can also use Homebrew:

```sh
brew install --cask devnull03/tap/qrate
```

qrate is free and open source. There is no account to create and nothing to sign in to. Projects
live on your computer.

## Look around the example first

When qrate opens, it shows the launcher. Before you create anything, click **Open example** under
**Explore an example project**. It opens four slides from a family collection, with photos to
browse and a few problems for qrate to point out. Nothing you do in it can hurt your own work, so
it is the place to click around.

To come back to the launcher later, choose **File ▸ Open Projects…** or press **Ctrl+O** (**Cmd+O**
on a Mac). It also lists your recent projects.

## Pick how you start

Under **Create New**, choose by what you already have, not by what you want to end up with. Any
project can take more rows, files and spreadsheets later.

| You have | Choose | What you get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A folder of scans and nothing written down | **Blank**, then link the folder | One row per file, nested in one row per folder |
| A spreadsheet somebody already started | **Spreadsheet + folder** | The spreadsheet as the grid, each row linked to its file |
| A shared Google Sheet | **Google Sheet** | A copy of the sheet as a project |
| Nothing yet | **Blank**, then **Add files later** | An empty grid to type into |

You can also drag things onto the launcher: a spreadsheet starts an import, and files or folders
start a folder-based project.

## Walk through the wizard

The wizard has a step for each decision, named across its top: **Name**, **Files**, **Link**,
**Columns** and **Create**. A blank project skips **Link**.

1. **Name.** Type a **Project name** and choose where to **Save project to**. qrate suggests the
   folder you last used, or a `qrate` folder in your Documents the first time.
2. **Files.** Choose your spreadsheet, your files folder, or both. **Add files later** starts
   with an empty table.
3. **Link.** For a spreadsheet and folder, qrate matches rows to files and tells you how many it
   found. See [Link an existing spreadsheet to its scans](/guides/link-scans-by-filename).
4. **Columns.** qrate needs to know two things: which column is each row's **Title**, and which
   holds its **File**. From a spreadsheet it guesses both. For a blank project, **Use default
   Title and File columns** gives you those two to start.
5. **Create.** The last page, **Ready to create your project**, sums it all up. For folders, it
   shows a **Hierarchy preview** of the rows it will make. Check it, then click **Create Project**.

The **Advanced** block on the Files step holds the archival description standard (RAD, DACS,
ISAD(G), Records in Contexts, or Custom) and what folders and files are called. Pick the
standard your institution uses. If you are not sure, any choice is safe: it can be changed later
in **Settings ▸ Project ▸ Description**, and changing it never rewrites rows you have described.
[Levels of description in a spreadsheet](/guides/levels-of-description-in-a-spreadsheet) explains
the choice.

Two facts shape everything after this:

- **The project is one file.** A `.qrate` file holds your rows, column settings, notes and
  history. Copy it to move or back up the project.
- **Your files stay where they are.** qrate links to your scans in their folder. It never moves
  them or copies them into the project, so keep that folder somewhere permanent.

qrate saves your edits on its own, a moment after you stop typing. A dot in the title bar means
there are changes it has not written yet, and **Ctrl+S** saves at once.
